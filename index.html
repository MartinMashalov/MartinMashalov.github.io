agent-fleet

Run thousands of concurrent LLM agent runs on a box that costs six dollars a month.

Most agent frameworks give every agent its own thread or process and fall over somewhere around fifty. But agent work is almost entirely spent waiting on a model API, so the right shape is one asyncio supervisor with a bounded pool. The hard parts are the ones that show up after that: a durable at-least-once queue with leases and idempotency keys so a redelivered step is not executed twice, per-step checkpointing so a killed process resumes mid-run instead of starting over, backpressure that never lets concurrency exceed its cap, and a shared token bucket with circuit breakers so a thousand agents don't stampede one provider. It ships with a deterministic mock model backend, so the benchmark reproduces without an API key or a dollar of spend.

gan-lab

GAN objectives are usually compared with cherry-picked samples. This one scores them against ground truth — twice, on two kinds of data.

Two testbeds, chosen because both have ground truth and neither needs a human to decide whether a sample "looks right." First a mixture of Gaussians, where the true modes are known exactly — that arm exists to validate the instrument, since a metric should be checked on data whose answer you already know. Then seventeen years of real intraday equity bars, where the stylized facts of financial returns are known and universal: fat tails, slowly decaying autocorrelation in absolute returns, near-zero autocorrelation in raw returns, the leverage effect, and kurtosis that falls under aggregation. Each becomes a number, estimated with a Hill tail index, ACF decay exponents and earth-mover distance. Crucially, every fact is first measured block-to-block on held-out real data, so a generator's deviation is judged against how much that statistic naturally moves — not against a point estimate. Five objectives — non-saturating, LSGAN, WGAN-GP, hinge with spectral normalisation and R1 — share one trainer, one budget and one set of seeds, over dilated causal convolutions.

Sen  papyra.org

An insurance agency describes a process in plain English. The system compiles it into a graph and then actually runs it — pausing for a human before anything leaves the building.

A plain-English description becomes a visual node graph — triggers, tool steps, loops, parallel branches, decisions, approval gates — and a durable engine executes that graph per account, reaching into Gmail and Outlook, Drive, Box, SharePoint and Dropbox, and the agency management system. The hard part is not the LLM; it is that a workflow which sends real client email must never send twice and must never silently skip a step. So every state transition goes through one chokepoint that enforces four invariants, each of which cites the production run that motivated it; cross-process safety uses transaction-scoped Postgres advisory locks with a compare-and-set relock, because PgBouncer's transaction pooling makes session-scoped locks a trap; and email fan-out claims its recipient group before sending, so redelivery cannot duplicate. I also wrote the canvas edge router — a visibility graph with Dijkstra over inflated card corners, drawn as Catmull-Rom splines — which took connection overlaps from eight to zero.

GridPull

Commercial insurance prospects built from public government filings — each one carrying a renewal date copied verbatim from a named source document.

An insurance producer needs to call a business roughly ninety days before its policy renews, and that date is buried in public filings. GridPull extracts it and cites where it came from. Full stack and end to end: FastAPI and Postgres behind PgBouncer, a React and TypeScript front end, a 150-worker asyncio pool streaming job progress over server-sent events, and two separate database engines — a pooled one with prepared statements disabled, and a NullPool engine that bypasses PgBouncer entirely because schema changes need session semantics that transaction pooling cannot give you. The cross-sell engine is deliberately deterministic with no model in the loop, because a coverage-gap recommendation has to be explainable. The document extractor scores its own output on field-fill, numeric-parse and date-parse rates, so accuracy is measured rather than asserted: across six families of real documents — invoices, annual reports, 10-K filings, insurance EOBs, purchase orders — its test suite reports 92% field fill at a 0% error rate.

AgentAlpha

Agents driving legacy enterprise software that has no API, through the browser, in production.

Some of the systems a business runs on were built before anyone thought to expose an endpoint. AgentAlpha drives them the way a person does: an execution manager works from a sitemap description of the target application and operates it through Playwright in the browser and PyWinAuto on the Windows desktop, with subagents that read the screen to find elements and a login manager that keeps sessions alive. Around that sits the machinery that makes it survivable in production — a continuous-drain worker loop with a dedicated pool per workload, Celery for background jobs, Redis task state that degrades honestly when a key expires, progress reporting that refuses to say "complete" while a step is still failing, per-run LLM cost accounting, and fallback across three model vendors so one outage doesn't stop the queue.

Research

MSc thesis

Dense rewards and unmasking policies in diffusion language models

MSc Econometrics, track Data Science  ·  University of Amsterdam, July 2026  ·  trained on Snellius (SURF) H100s

Diffusion language models write text by repeatedly un-masking a sequence rather than left to right. That gives them a choice an autoregressive model never faces: which position to reveal next. So when you post-train one with reinforcement learning, there are two things you could be changing — the model's weights, or its decoding order. My thesis asks which, and finds that the answer is neither on its own.

A full factorial: four models (LLaDA-8B, Dream-7B, and two DiffuCoder-7B checkpoints) × two methods (LoRA diffu-GRPO on the weights, and a learned unmasking-order policy over frozen weights) × two reward designs (binary pass/fail, and a partial-credit reward built from test-pass fraction, AST similarity and syntax validity). Measured on HumanEval, MATH-500, MBPP and GSM8K.

The headlineFitting the ordering policy and the weights together beats either alone. Dream's HumanEval pass@1 goes 38.4 → 46.7 with an order policy, → 43.3 if you carry a stale policy across, and → 61.8 ±0.7 when the policy is re-fitted on the merged model. On LLaDA it wins 6 of 6 paired seeds, +2.86pp pooled, p < 0.01, and halves seed variance.
An honest nullPartial-credit rewards make the training signal 2–7× denser and do not, by themselves, raise accuracy. Denser supervision turns out not to mean better supervision.
Where density paysAt the ordering stage instead: +8.5pp on HumanEval for Dream over a binary reward, and where the binary reward collapsed on one seed, partial credit stayed stable across all three.
Too trained to trainAn already RL-tuned checkpoint resists every method. Its within-group reward spread is 0.07–0.16 against 0.20–0.41 for the others, leaving 11–15% effective GRPO groups and almost no advantage signal. The identical recipe on its supervised-only sibling gave 2.4× the reward spread and 2.6× the effective groups — which implicates the earlier RL stage, not the architecture.
Reported honestlyWilson intervals on every figure, generated from one matrix file that is the single source of truth. Single-seed cells are labelled exploratory rather than quoted as results. I also found and fixed a scorer bug in the baseline that counted any parseable code as a pass.
